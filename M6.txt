
N-gram 
An n-gram is just a sequence of n items (usually words or characters) taken from a text.

what are N-grams
    An N-gram is a sequence of N consecutive words.
    Examples:

    Unigram (1-gram): “I”, “am”, “happy”
    Bigram (2-gram): “I am”, “am happy”
    Trigram (3-gram): “I am happy”

why do we use N-grams ?
 to predict next words or compute sentence probability 
 
applications :
 google search suggestions (these predictions are based on how frequently (n-grams) appear in huge text data)
 email writing (suggestion based on common pairs found in language models)
 autocomplete 
 spell correction 
 speech recognition 
 machine translation 
 text generation

 N-grams help capture lexical and local syntactic patterns.

//N-grams are mainly used to predict the next word by checking which word
 (or sequence of words) occurs most frequently after the given context.

 What N-gram Models Do
    N-gram models try to guess the next word based on how often word sequences appear in a text dataset.

Different N-gram Models
 
 1. Unigram (1-word model)
    Ignores previous words.
    Predict the next word based on overall frequency in the whole dataset.

    Example:
    “the” (highest frequency)
    “is”
    “and”

    But → unigram gives poor predictions because it doesn’t consider context.
    
 2. Bigram (2-word model)
    Predicts next word using previous 1 word.

    Formula:
    P(next | previous) = Count(previous, next) / Count(previous)

    Example:
    "good ➝ morning" (40 times)
    "good ➝ job" (10 times)

    So after “good”, it will suggest “morning”.

  3. Trigram (3-word model)
        Uses previous 2 words for prediction.

        Example:
        After “I am going”, the most common next word might be “to”. 
        
   .

🤖 How suggestions are generated?
    When typing something like:

    "I am going" → ?
    The system:
    1. Looks at all trigrams starting with “I am going”.
    2. If not found, it backs off to bigram: “am going”.
    3. If still not found, it backs off to unigram.
    4. Picks the word with maximum probability.

    This is called backoff or smoothing.


why we need probabilistic language models ?
 applications :

  Examples:

    P(high winds tonight) > P(large winds tonight)  - machine translation 
    P(fifteen minutes) > P(fifteen minuets)  - spell correction

//language models 
  two main tasks 
   1. P(sentence) - probability of entire sequence 
       P(W) = P(w1,w2,w3,w4.....wn)
   2. P(next word | previous words)
       P(w5 | w1,w2,w3,w4) 
  Any model that does this = language model.


//chain rules 
 P(x1,x2,x3,........xn) = P(x1) P(x2|x1) P(x3|x1,x2) P(x4|x1,x2,x3) ...... P(xn|x1,.....xn-1)
   ex: P("its water is so transparent) = p(its) * p(water|its) * p(is|its water) *p(so|its water is) * P(transparent| its water is so)

 P(next | previous) = Count(previous, next) / Count(previous)
   ex: P(the | its water is so transparent that) = 
                Count(its water is so transparent that the)/ Count(its water is so transparent)


 //markovs assumption 
    P(w1​…wn​) ≈ ∏ P(wi​∣wi−k​…wi−1​)
     
     ex:  P(the∣its water is so transparent that) ≈ P(the∣that) or P(the∣transparent that) (means it has only last 1 or 2 words matter)

    k=1 -> bigram 
    k=2 -> trigram 

    unigram - P(w1w2w3​…wn​) ~ P(wi)
    bigram -  P(w1w2w3​…wn​) ~ P(wi|wi-1)    Condition on previous word:
    
    Example sentence: “This is a sentence”
    Unigrams: This, is, a, sentence
    Bigrams: This is, is a, a sentence
    Trigrams: This is a, is a sentence

    Even 4-grams/5-grams cannot capture long-distance relationships

    unigram model : P(w1)P(w2)P(w3).....
    bigram model : P(w1)P(w2|w1)P(w3|w2)P(w4|w3)
    trigram model : P(w1)P(w2|w1)P(w3|w2,w1).....P(wn|wn-1wn-2)
    N-gram model : P(w1)P(w2|w1).....P(wn|wn-1wn-2......wn-N)

limitations of N-grams
